We Don’t Need More Information. We Need Better Ways to Act.

Imagine this: you are scrolling through your phone on your way to work when you come across a video about a crisis happening somewhere in the world. You stop, watch it, read the comments, and perhaps share it with your friends. Later that day, you see another post about a demonstration taking place in your city. You agree with the cause and want to attend, but then you start looking for basic information. Where is it happening? Who organized it? What time does it start? Is it still happening? Before long, you are moving between Instagram posts, group chats, websites, and old announcements trying to piece everything together. Eventually, you give up and continue with your day.

It is easy to assume that someone who does this simply did not care enough to act. But that is often not the reality. The person cared enough to stop scrolling in the first place. What they lacked was not concern, but a clear path from that concern to participation.

The gap between knowing and doing

This is one of the challenges created by the way we experience social issues online. We have never had more access to information. A crisis can reach us within minutes, an investigation can spread across platforms overnight, and an organization can reach thousands of potential supporters with a single post. Yet having access to information does not necessarily mean knowing what to do with it.

Someone might want to support a local organization but have no idea how to volunteer. Another person might have useful skills in design, writing, translation, or organizing but never find a community that needs them. Someone else might want to attend a demonstration or community event but discover that the information is scattered across several platforms, buried beneath newer posts, or no longer available.

The information may exist, but finding it can require time, effort, and familiarity with networks that newcomers simply do not have. For someone who is already overwhelmed by everything happening in the world, that additional friction can be enough to stop them from participating.

Caring does not always come with a roadmap

Consider the person who sees an announcement for a peaceful demonstration and genuinely wants to attend. Their questions are simple: Who is organizing it? Where should they go? When does it start? What should they expect? Who else will be there?

These questions do not indicate a lack of commitment. They are the normal questions of someone trying to take their first step.

The same is true for other forms of participation. Action does not always mean standing at the front of a demonstration. It might mean joining a teach-in, volunteering at a community organization, supporting mutual aid, offering professional skills, attending a local meeting, or helping distribute information. Different people have different capacities, interests, and circumstances. The challenge is finding the opportunity that fits.

When those opportunities are difficult to discover, people can remain stuck in the same cycle: they become informed, they care, they want to help, and then they do not know where to begin.

From information to participation

There is also something important about knowing that you are not alone. When an issue feels enormous, individual action can seem insignificant. Seeing thousands of people discussing something online can paradoxically make a person feel more isolated, especially when there is no obvious way to connect that online conversation with meaningful participation in the real world.

But movements are rarely built by everyone doing the same thing. They are built by people contributing in different ways. One person organizes an event, another volunteers, another provides a skill, another supports mutual aid, and someone else simply decides to show up and bring a friend.

Technology can either make this process more difficult by overwhelming people with information, or it can help make participation easier to discover. The opportunity is not necessarily to give people more content to consume, but to help them navigate from awareness toward action.

There should be a clearer bridge between seeing something and doing something about it; between discovering that people are organizing and finding out how to participate; between feeling powerless and realizing that there are already people taking action around you.

Where JayWalk comes in

This is the gap JayWalk aims to help bridge.

JayWalk is a privacy-first platform designed to help people discover real-world movements and opportunities for participation. Instead of simply adding another stream of content to an already crowded internet, JayWalk focuses on helping people find what is happening, discover ways to participate, connect with organizers and communities, and take that next step.

Because awareness matters. Information matters. But they should not have to be where the journey ends.

The goal is simple: help people move from knowing that something is happening to finding a way to be part of what happens next.
