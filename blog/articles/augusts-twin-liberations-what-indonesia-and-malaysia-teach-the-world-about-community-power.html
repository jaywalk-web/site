August's Twin Liberations: What Indonesia and Malaysia Teach the World About Community Power

We began this series by remembering August as a month of activism, remembrance, and collective action. Now, we turn to two specific milestones, August 17 and August 31, that mark the birth of two modern Southeast Asian nations: Indonesia and Malaysia. For an international audience, these are not just national holidays; they are masterclasses in how ordinary people, united by a shared vision, can dismantle empires. And in their struggles, we find echoes of another August tradition, one born in U.S. prisons, rooted in the same fight against systemic oppression.

August 17, 1945: Indonesia's Proclamation That Became a People's Movement

On the morning of August 17, 1945, in the aftermath of World War II, a declaration of independence was read in Jakarta, signaling the birth of the Republic of Indonesia. It was a moment that officially ended centuries of Dutch colonial rule, but more importantly, it was a spark that ignited a nationwide, grassroots uprising. What makes this date remarkable for any student of community organizing is that the proclamation did not create the movement; the movement created the proclamation.

Within hours of the declaration, it was not the military or the political elite who carried the message across the vast Indonesian archipelago; it was the youth. Young people, many of whom had been involved in underground networks, took to the streets of Jakarta and beyond. They seized communication infrastructure, occupied government buildings, and spread the news via newspapers, radio broadcasts, and human chains. They did not wait for instructions. They became the instructions themselves.

In cities and remote villages across Indonesia, local communities spontaneously formed struggle units. They collected weapons, raised the national flag, and established local administrations. This was not a top-down coup; it was a decentralized, organic uprising where every neighborhood became a node of resistance. From Sumatra to Sulawesi, from Java to Papua, ordinary Indonesians refused to accept the return of Dutch rule. For an international observer, the lesson is clear: lasting liberation cannot be dictated from a podium. It must be cultivated in the streets, the marketplaces, and the living rooms of ordinary citizens. Indonesia's revolution succeeded because it was owned by everyone, not just the generals or the politicians.

August 31, 1957: Malaysia's Moment When Diverse Communities Became One

Twelve years after Indonesia's proclamation, on August 31, 1957, another declaration of independence took effect, this time in the Federation of Malaya, which would later become Malaysia. This was the culmination of decades of negotiation and cross-cultural cooperation against British colonial rule. What makes Malaysia's independence equally instructive is the demographic reality of the nation: it was, and is, a multi-ethnic, multi-religious society. The drive for self-governance did not come from a single homogenous group, but from a coalition of communities including indigenous Malays, Chinese laborers, Indian plantation workers, and various indigenous groups from the eastern regions of the country.

The path to Malaysian independence required these communities to set aside their differences and recognize a common enemy: British colonial domination. They shared resources, coordinated protests, and built trust across cultural lines. When the moment of liberation finally arrived, the streets of the capital were filled with people of every background, shoulder to shoulder, not as separate groups, but as a single community. Thousands gathered in cities across the country to witness the lowering of the colonial flag. People of every race and background filled the fields, hearts beating as one. There were no divisions, only joy and pride that they were finally free.

For an international audience, Malaysia's independence is a vital reminder: true liberation is intersectional. Struggles for justice are rarely won by a single demographic acting alone. They require coalition-building, empathy across cultural lines, and a shared commitment to a future that benefits everyone. The ability of Malaysia's diverse communities to organize together was not just a nice ideal; it was a strategic necessity that made independence possible.

Parallel Struggles, Shared DNA

Though separated by geography and colonial history, the Indonesian and Malaysian independence movements share a common DNA with each other and with other liberation struggles around the world, including the tradition of Black August. While separated by context, they are united by fundamental principles that transcend borders.

The first is resistance to extraction. The Dutch exploited Indonesia's spices, labor, and natural resources for centuries. The British profited from Malaya's rubber and tin, built on the backs of imported laborers from China and India. Similarly, the transatlantic slave trade and its aftermath built a system of racial capitalism in the United States. All these systems treated human beings and their lands as resources to be exploited. Resistance, in all cases, began with the refusal to be treated as commodities.

The second is the use of underground networks. Indonesia's youth relied on secret radio broadcasts and word-of-mouth chains to spread the proclamation of independence across thousands of islands. Malaysian organizers held covert meetings in plantation shacks and fishing villages. These were the communication infrastructures of liberation, built outside the watchful eye of Dutch and British colonial authorities, sustained by trust and shared purpose. The same dynamic played out in the prison cells and clandestine study groups of Black August, where organizers used coded letters and underground newspapers to educate and mobilize.

The third is the colonial strategy of divide and rule, and the refusal to be divided. The Dutch deliberately fractured Indonesian communities along ethnic, religious, and regional lines. The British employed similar tactics in Malaya, pitting ethnic groups against one another to maintain control. The response, in both Southeast Asian nations and among Black communities in the United States, was the same: communities chose solidarity over division. They recognized that their common oppressor was more powerful than their differences.

The fourth is the role of youth as the vanguard. In Indonesia, it was the young who seized the moment and forced the declaration of independence ahead of schedule. In Malaysia, young people from every ethnic background filled the streets. Across all liberation movements, it is often the young, those with the most at stake in the future, who refuse to accept the world as it is and dare to imagine what it could be.

Finally, there is the principle of memory as a weapon. For Indonesia and Malaysia, independence day celebrations are ritual reenactments of the moment communities reclaimed their destiny. The flag-raising, the recitation of the proclamation, the retelling of stories, these are acts of collective memory that keep the spirit of resistance alive. Black August operates on the same principle, insisting that the stories of those who fought, and those who are still fighting, must not be erased. This insistence on remembrance, on refusing to let the past be erased, is a common thread that runs through all liberation movements, from Jakarta to Kuala Lumpur to California.

From the Colonial Era to the Digital Era

In the 1940s and 50s, Indonesia's freedom fighters organized using typewriters, printing presses, shortwave radios, and word of mouth. Malaysia's independence movement relied on meeting halls, community centers, and trusted networks that crossed ethnic and religious lines. Today, the tools have evolved, but the fundamental need remains the same: communities need safe, reliable ways to communicate and coordinate.

In modern movements, a smartphone can be a printing press. A messaging app can be a secret meeting hall. A live map can track threats and opportunities in real-time. However, the digital age introduces new vulnerabilities: surveillance, misinformation, digital harassment, and platform censorship. The faster information moves, the faster it can be weaponized. A database that empowers organizers can also endanger them if it falls into the wrong hands. The same threats that Dutch and British colonial authorities once posed, infiltration, surveillance, censorship, and repression, now exist in digital form, often amplified by algorithms and corporate interests. The tools of liberation must evolve to meet them.

JayWalk: Building on the Legacy of Indonesian and Malaysian Liberation

The underground networks of Indonesia in 1945, the radio operators, the pamphlet distributors, the couriers running between villages across the archipelago, were the communication infrastructure of liberation. The community meetings in Malaysia's plantation shacks and fishing villages, where Chinese, Malay, and Indian organizers built trust across cultural divides, were the trust infrastructure of resistance. The study groups and clandestine gatherings that sustained Black August were the knowledge infrastructure of survival. Today, that infrastructure is digital. And it faces the same threats: surveillance, censorship, infiltration, and erasure.

This is precisely why JayWalk exists. When Indonesia's freedom fighters needed to coordinate across thousands of islands, they built human chains. JayWalk builds digital chains, encrypted, organizer-controlled, and accessible. When Malaysia's diverse communities needed to build trust across ethnic and religious lines, they shared meals and held open meetings. JayWalk creates secure digital spaces where diverse groups can convene without fear of infiltration or doxxing. When Black August organizers needed to protect the identities of political prisoners and their families, they used codes and aliases. JayWalk uses end-to-end encryption and privacy-first design to ensure that identities are protected, and that sensitive information remains in trusted hands.

JayWalk is not just an app. It is the modern-day crosswalk for communities navigating hostile terrain, whether that terrain is a colonial plantation in Malaysia, a Dutch-controlled island in Indonesia, a segregated city in the United States, a maximum-security prison, or an algorithm that silences dissent. It is built on the understanding that the right to assemble and organize is fundamental, and that technology should serve communities, not the other way around.

What It Means to Be a J-Walker in a Global Struggle

If you walk with communities, you recognize that the fight against Dutch colonialism in Indonesia, British imperialism in Malaysia, and racial apartheid in the United States are branches of the same tree. A J-Walker understands that oppression is intersectional, and so is liberation. A J-Walker in Jakarta, a J-Walker in Kuala Lumpur, and a J-Walker anywhere else in the world share a common mission: they organize not just for themselves, but for the collective; they protect not just their own safety, but the safety of their entire network; they remember because forgetting is the first victory of the oppressor; and they connect across borders, across languages, across movements, because they know that solidarity is not optional; it is strategic.

Being a J-Walker means refusing to let distance, whether geographic or historical, divide you from the broader human struggle for dignity. It means recognizing that the teenager who ran through Jakarta's streets in 1945, the rubber tapper who joined a secret meeting in Malaysia in 1957, and the political prisoner who studied in a U.S. cell in 1971 are all part of the same long walk toward justice. It means understanding that liberation is not a destination, but a journey, and that the only way to walk it is together.

The Walk Continues

This August, as we remember the heroes of Indonesia and Malaysia and the martyrs of Black August, let us not stop at remembrance. Let us walk. Learn the histories that connect these struggles across the Pacific and beyond. Share knowledge responsibly, because misinformation is the enemy of liberation. Protect your communities, online and offline, using tools designed with safety in mind. Build bridges across movements, because no one is free until everyone is free.

JayWalk provides the infrastructure. But it is the J-Walkers, the organizers, the humanitarians, the allies, the dreamers, who bring it to life. When your community is ready to organize, mobilize, and connect, whether you are standing in the shadow of a colonial monument in Jakarta, a plantation house in Malaysia, a prison wall in California, or a digital firewall, JayWalk is here to walk alongside you.

Organize. Mobilize. Connect.

From the streets of Jakarta to the plantations of Malaya, from the cells of California to the digital frontier, the walk for freedom is one. And we take it together.
