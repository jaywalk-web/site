Finding Connection in a Disconnected World

The internet was built around the promise of connection. It was supposed to make distance smaller, bring people closer, and give us new ways to communicate with one another. Yet there is something deeply ironic about the digital world we have built: we can be connected to thousands of people and still feel completely alone.

Perhaps the digital ecosystem is a little like a kitchen knife. A knife can be used to prepare a meal, create something useful, or help someone, but in the wrong hands it can also be used to cause harm. The object itself does not make the decision. Its impact depends on how it is used and what we choose to do with it.

The same can be said about the internet. Social media platforms and digital tools can help us learn, communicate, organize, discover new perspectives, and find people we might never have encountered otherwise. But they can also keep us trapped in cycles of outrage, comparison, fear, and endless consumption. An algorithm can introduce us to a community that changes our life, or it can keep showing us increasingly distressing content because it knows we will continue watching. The important thing is to remember that the algorithm does not have to decide what our attention becomes.

When connection becomes consumption

Every day, we move through an endless stream of messages, posts, videos, headlines, and conversations. We can see what someone across the world is experiencing within seconds, follow communities we have never met, and watch events unfold from thousands of miles away. But watching is not always connecting.

Doomscrolling is a good example. Staying informed matters, but constantly consuming distressing stories, crises, arguments, and traumatic content can leave some people feeling anxious, exhausted, overwhelmed, or hopeless. For people who are already struggling, repeatedly encountering emotionally intense content can sometimes intensify distress or bring difficult thoughts and experiences closer to the surface. The feed can make the entire world feel like it is happening to us at once, and because there is always another post, another video, and another update, there is rarely a natural moment when we are told to stop. Sometimes, we have to choose when to step away.

Digital spaces can also help us connect

The answer is not to reject technology. The same digital ecosystem that can contribute to unhealthy patterns can also create opportunities for learning, creativity, solidarity, and connection. Social media like Upscrolled can give people opportunities to discover ideas, conversations, perspectives, and communities rather than simply encouraging endless consumption. The purpose of a digital space does not have to be keeping us in front of a screen for as long as possible. Technology can also help us discover something worth taking beyond the screen.

That distinction matters because a platform can give us another thing to consume, or it can help us find something to participate in. It can reinforce isolation, or it can become a doorway to other people. What matters is how we use these spaces and what they ultimately lead us toward.

We are more than what the algorithm shows us

Algorithms are designed to keep us engaged. That does not automatically make them bad. They can help us discover music, knowledge, people, causes, communities, and opportunities that genuinely enrich our lives. But engagement is not the same thing as wellbeing.

The content that keeps us watching is not necessarily the content that makes us feel better, learn more deeply, or live more meaningfully. Sometimes the healthiest choice is to close the app, put the phone down, and do something the algorithm cannot measure. We can talk to someone, go outside, attend an event, learn something new, make something, or meet people. The digital world can be part of our lives without becoming the place where our entire life happens.

Connection means meeting people

There is a difference between reading about a community and sitting in a room with the people who make up that community. There is a difference between watching a discussion and having someone ask what you think. There is a difference between seeing a call for volunteers and actually showing up somewhere and working alongside others.

These experiences can give us something that endless scrolling cannot: a sense of presence. Meeting people can introduce us to new ideas, interests, skills, and perspectives. It can give us opportunities to learn, contribute, laugh, listen, and simply spend time around others. Sometimes wellbeing can begin with something as ordinary as having somewhere to go and people who are glad you came. We do not necessarily need thousands of followers. Sometimes we need a few people who know us.

Community and suicide prevention

During Suicide Prevention Month, conversations often focus on recognizing when someone may be struggling and encouraging people to seek appropriate help. Those conversations are essential. But prevention can also involve creating environments where people have opportunities to connect, belong, and feel that their presence matters.

Community is not a substitute for professional mental-health care, crisis support, or treatment, and no individual should be expected to carry another person's crisis alone. But meaningful social connection can still be an important part of wellbeing. A weekly gathering, a shared interest, a volunteer group, a creative community, a place to learn, or simply a few people who notice when you have not shown up can create something that a feed cannot: a feeling of belonging.

When the digital world makes everything feel overwhelming, real-world community can remind us that we are not simply observers of the world. We are participants in it.

Take back your attention

We cannot control every algorithm, every headline, or everything that appears on our screens, but we can become more intentional about what we give our attention to. We can choose to learn instead of endlessly consume, question instead of simply react, and close the feed when it begins affecting our wellbeing. We can also choose to turn something we discovered online into a conversation, a new skill, a community, or meaningful action.

The digital ecosystem gives us enormous power. Like any powerful tool, that power can be used constructively or destructively. The choice is not always completely within our control, and mental-health struggles are never simply a matter of making better choices. But we do have agency in how we engage with technology, where we direct our attention, and whether we allow the feed to become our entire experience of the world.

Using technology to find one another

This is where JayWalk comes in. JayWalk is built around the idea that technology should not only help us discover information; it should also help us discover people, communities, movements, and opportunities to participate.

Instead of endlessly consuming information about an issue, JayWalk can help people move toward the communities already doing something about it. People can discover events, find movements, learn what is happening, and identify ways to participate in the real world.

In that sense, JayWalk and social media like Upscrolled represent a different possibility for the digital ecosystem. They do not have to compete with the internet's ability to inform us; they can help us use that information differently. The question becomes less about how long we can keep someone online and more about what someone can discover because they were online.

Maybe they discover an idea. Maybe they learn something new. Maybe they find a community. Maybe they meet someone who changes their perspective. Maybe they find a place where they finally feel that they belong.

Technology should not become the thing that decides our wellbeing for us. It should be a tool we learn to use intentionally, with enough awareness to recognize when something is helping us and when we need to step away.

The internet can keep us watching, or it can help us find somewhere to belong.

This Suicide Prevention Month, let us remember that wellbeing is not only about what we consume. It is also about who we connect with, what we learn, where we belong, and how we choose to participate in the world around us.

The algorithm does not get the final say. We do.
