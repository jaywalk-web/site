August, Remembered: A Month of Activism, Community & Collective Action

Welcome to August—a month of remembrance, resistance, solidarity, and communities finding ways to be heard.

Every August, communities around the world remember different histories, struggles, cultures, and humanitarian causes. Some of these occasions are formally recognized by the United Nations and other international institutions, while others have emerged directly from communities themselves—from histories that were preserved not because powerful institutions remembered them, but because ordinary people refused to let them disappear.

For those engaged in activism, humanitarian work, community organizing, and movements for justice, August offers something more than a collection of dates on a calendar. It is an opportunity to reflect on the people and movements that came before us, the communities still facing challenges today, and the ways we can connect with one another to create meaningful change.

Black August: Memory as Resistance

One of the most important examples is Black August, a month-long tradition rooted in the history of Black liberation movements and political prisoners in the United States. It emerged as a period of remembrance and political education connected to the life and death of George Jackson and the broader history of Black resistance. Over time, Black August has become associated with remembering freedom fighters, political prisoners, revolutionary movements, and struggles against racial oppression.

Its significance extends beyond a particular historical moment. It reminds us that memory itself can become a form of resistance.

Communities have always needed ways to preserve stories, communicate with one another, share knowledge, and organize around a common purpose. Before smartphones and social networks, this happened through meetings, newspapers, pamphlets, letters, community centers, churches, unions, radio, word of mouth, and networks of trusted people.

The tools have changed. The need has not.

August Is a Calendar of Communities

Look at the international calendar and a pattern begins to emerge. August is filled with reminders about people, communities, histories, and the consequences of losing the ability to protect them. Each observance highlights a different part of the human experience, but together they raise a larger question: How do we protect the dignity, identity, memory, and ability of communities to participate in the world around them?

  • 1–7 August — World Breastfeeding Week
    The 2026 theme is “Breastfeeding for a sustainable start in life: strengthen what works.” It is a reminder that community support begins with something fundamental: people caring for one another.
  • 6 August — International Day of Awareness of the Special Development Needs and Challenges of Landlocked Developing Countries
    Highlights how geography can shape opportunity, and that global cooperation matters when communities face structural barriers.
  • 6 August — Hiroshima Remembrance
    Raises questions about war, civilian life, peace, and the consequences of technological power.
  • 9 August — International Day of the World's Indigenous Peoples
    The 2026 theme, “Honouring Indigenous Midwives: Safeguarding Life and Well-being,” highlights Indigenous knowledge systems and the transmission of knowledge across generations.
  • 9 August — Nagasaki Remembrance
    Together with Hiroshima, reminds us that technology is never simply “good” or “bad” — its impact depends on how people choose to use power.
  • 11 August — World Steelpan Day
    Celebrates steelpan culture and the ways communities preserve identity through creativity and shared expression.
  • 12 August — International Youth Day
    Recognizes that young people are already organizing, creating, and shaping communities, increasingly through digital technologies.
  • 17 August — Indonesian Independence Day
    A reminder that collective identity, solidarity, and the ability of people to determine their own future are deeply connected.
  • 19 August — World Humanitarian Day
    Recognizes humanitarian workers and the role of documentation — photographs, messages, maps, and digital archives — in preserving evidence and testimony.
  • 21 August — International Day of Remembrance and Tribute to the Victims of Terrorism
    Reminds us that behind every statistic are people, families, and communities whose lives can be permanently changed by violence.
  • 22 August — Victims of Acts of Violence Based on Religion or Belief
    Highlights the importance of respectful plurality: communities can hold different beliefs while still deserving dignity and safety.
  • 23 August — International Day for the Remembrance of the Slave Trade and Its Abolition
    Connects historical memory with the struggle against slavery and the movements that challenged systems of oppression.
  • 27 August — World Lake Day
    Recognizes the importance of lakes and the communities and ecosystems connected to them.
  • 29 August — International Day against Nuclear Tests
    Calls attention to the dangers of nuclear testing and the question: what happens when technology becomes more powerful than our ability to govern its consequences?
  • 30 August — International Day of the Victims of Enforced Disappearances
    Particularly significant for questions of documentation, accountability, and memory. Digital tools can help preserve information, but must be designed with safety and dignity in mind.
  • 31 August — International Day for People of African Descent
    Closes the month with recognition of identity, history, equality, and the contributions of people of African descent.

From the Street to the Screen

There is another important thread running through these stories: how communities communicate. Communities have always developed their own ways of organizing — meetings, newspapers, pamphlets, letters, community centers, radio, word of mouth, and trusted networks.

Today, a smartphone can become a communication hub. A messaging platform can connect organizers across borders. A live map can help communities understand what is happening around them. Social networks can take a local story and bring it to a global audience within minutes.

Technology has not created community. It has changed the ways communities can connect. But that transformation comes with responsibility: the faster information moves, the faster misinformation can move too. A platform that gives people a voice can also expose them to harassment. A database designed to help organizers can become dangerous if sensitive information falls into the wrong hands.

This Is Where JayWalk Steps In

JayWalk was built around a simple principle: the right to assemble is fundamental. Yet organizing today can be complicated by censorship, misinformation, surveillance, digital harassment, and repression. Communities need technology that does more than simply allow people to communicate — they need tools designed with their safety, accessibility, privacy, and ability to organize in mind.

JayWalk is a secure platform for activists and allies worldwide, designed to help communities organize, mobilize, communicate, and support nonviolent movements while maintaining greater control over their digital spaces. With Version 1 now available, JayWalk brings together tools designed around the realities of modern organizing: secure and encrypted communication, global community building, organizer-controlled access, and safety maps.

Built for Communities, Guided by Principles

Developed in collaboration with Tech for Palestine, JayWalk supports technology for movements facing digital suppression and prioritizes causes that amplify oppressed and marginalized communities. JayWalk recognizes that struggles for justice are interconnected — climate justice, racial equity, Indigenous sovereignty, gender equality, humanitarian causes, and other liberation movements can build solidarity across borders.

The platform is intended for nonviolent resistance. Its purpose is to help people organize and connect — not to create another space for oppression.

So, Who Is a J-Walker?

A J-Walker is anyone who believes communities should have the freedom and ability to connect, communicate, organize, and stand together safely and peacefully. J-Walkers can be activists, organizers, humanitarian workers, volunteers, allies, or community members from anywhere in the world.

What connects them is a commitment to justice, dignity, solidarity, and the protection of communities facing oppression or marginalization. Being a J-Walker means walking with communities, not speaking over them — listening to people directly affected, respecting different perspectives, and supporting nonviolent movements working toward justice, equity, and liberation.

August Is Where We Begin

August gives us a reason to pause and remember, but remembrance does not have to end with looking backward. We can learn from the movements that came before us. We can listen to communities experiencing challenges today. We can use technology more responsibly. We can create safer ways to communicate. We can build bridges across borders and support people whose voices might otherwise be ignored.

Whether you are an activist, organizer, humanitarian, volunteer, technologist, community member, or simply someone who believes that people deserve to be heard, there is a place for you in this conversation.

Explore the August calendar. Learn about the histories behind these observances. Listen to the communities connected to them. Share knowledge responsibly. Find ways to contribute.

And when your community is ready to organize, mobilize, and connect, JayWalk is here to walk alongside you.
